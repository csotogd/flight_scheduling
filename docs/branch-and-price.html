Act I · The business problem

1Four departments, one leaky pipeline

A cargo airline sells exactly one product: a promise — your freight, picked up here, delivered there, by Thursday. Behind that promise sit four decisions, and in almost every airline they are made by four different teams, one after another, each treating the previous team's answer as fixed:

1 · Network planning

Which flights should exist next season?

2 · Fleet planning

Which aircraft type flies each one?

3 · Operations

Can our actual tails chain those flights — and still make maintenance?

4 · Revenue management

Which shipments do we accept, and how do they route?

The problem is that these decisions are one decision wearing four hats. A flight is only worth flying if the right aircraft can reach it and there is cargo to fill it — but the team deciding the flight doesn't see the cargo, and the team seeing the cargo can no longer change the flight. Where does the value come from, in manager terms? Fuller airplanes (the same tails carry more paying tonnes), fewer lost promises (demand that used to be stranded gets a routing — or a deliberately priced subcontract), capex avoidance (the integrator-scale design uses 111 of 148 aircraft — the schedule tells you how many airplanes you actually need), and planner leverage (the tool tries 600 candidate flights per design run; no planning department can evaluate that honestly by hand, because every candidate interacts with every other).

Act II · From business to math

2The vocabulary: eight objects, drawn

Before any math, the nouns. These eight objects are the whole problem — every constraint, variable and algorithm below is just these objects meeting each other. After this section, nothing on this page should feel foreign.

Airport — hub or spoke

Hubs run the night sort: cargo lands, is re-sorted, and departs on a different flight. Spokes just send and receive. Transfers (and maintenance) happen at hubs.

Leg

One takeoff and one landing: A→B, with a departure minute, a duration, and a distance. The atom of the schedule — capacity is consumed leg by leg.

Flight

A scheduled service, possibly chaining legs (A→B→C). Mandatory flights are promises already made; optional flights are candidates the model may choose to create — or not.

Fleet

An aircraft type: payload (tonnes and m³), range (shrinking payload as legs get longer), speed, costs, maintenance rules — and a count. You own a finite number of each.

Rotation / flight string

One aircraft's entire week: a chain of flights with turn times between them, returning to where it started (the week repeats!), visiting maintenance often enough.

O&D demand ("origin & destination")

A customer's shipment: from where, to where, how many tonnes and m³, when it's ready, its delivery deadline, and the rate paid per tonne. A big instance has thousands.

Itinerary / cargo path

The route one shipment actually takes: a sequence of legs with transfers at hubs, respecting ready time, deadline, transfer minima and handling times.

The periodic week

The schedule repeats every week (10,080 minutes), so time is a circle: a rotation or an itinerary may cross Sunday midnight and continue into "next" Monday — which is this Monday.

3Write it down: a mixed-integer program

Optimization people model decisions like this as a mixed-integer program (MIP): pick decision variables (numbers the solver may choose), a linear objective to maximize, and linear constraints the choices must respect — where some variables are required to be whole numbers. That last requirement is the entire difficulty and the entire point: "0.4 airplanes" is not a thing, and integer-or-not is what separates problems solvable in milliseconds from problems that are NP-hard.

Three families of variables describe an airline's whole week:

xp ≥ 0 · continuous

Tonnes of a shipment flowing on itinerary p. Cargo may split across routes, so fractions are fine.

ys ∈ {0, 1, 2, …} · integer

How many aircraft fly work-week s. You can fly a rotation twice with two tails — but never 0.4 times.

zf ∈ {0, 1} · binary

Does optional flight f get created at all? The network-design decision itself, as a variable.

The model, line by line (a lightly simplified ACSP-T — the real rows are (14)–(22) in the paper, dissected interactively in §7):

maximize  Σp revp·xp − Σs costs·ys Profit. Revenue of every routed tonne, minus the operating cost of every aircraft work-week we choose to fly.
Σs ∋ f ys = 1   ∀ mandatory flight f Cover. Every flight already promised to customers is operated by exactly one aircraft's work-week. Non-negotiable.
Σs ∋ f ys = zf   ∀ optional flight f Selection linking. An optional flight is operated if and only if we decided it exists. Flight selection and rotation planning, welded together.
Σs of fleet k ys ≤ nk   ∀ fleet k Fleet count. The chosen 777F work-weeks can't need more than the 26 airframes we own. Fleet assignment lives here.
Σp ∋ ℓ xp ≤ Σs covering ℓ capℓ,k·ys   ∀ leg ℓ Capacity — the coupling row. Cargo on a leg must fit in the airplanes actually flying it. capℓ,k comes from the payload-range curve, so long legs offer fewer tonnes. The only place x meets y — and the row that makes the four decisions one problem. (Same again for volume, in m³.)
Σp of od xp ≤ dod   ∀ shipment od Demand. Ship at most what the customer tendered ("=" in deliver-all mode, where everything must move — outsourced if necessary).
x ≥ 0,  y integer,  z binary Domains. Tonnes may split; airplanes and flights may not. This line is why the problem is hard.

Notice what's missing: no constraint says "itineraries must connect", "transfers need 90 minutes", "rotations must return to base", "the A-check is due every 40 cycles". They're not forgotten — they're hidden inside the variables: p only ranges over itineraries that are already feasible, s only over work-weeks that already obey turn times and maintenance. That's the path-flow trick (§6), and it has a price: p and s range over trillions of feasible stories. You cannot even write this matrix down, let alone hand it to a solver.

4Branch & price & cut, from zero

If you've never met these words, here is the 90-second version — four ideas, each fixing the previous one's weakness. The rest of the page is just these four ideas, done carefully.

1 · The LP relaxation

Drop the "whole numbers only" rule and a MIP becomes a linear program — solvable blisteringly fast even with millions of variables. The catch: its optimum sits at a corner like "fly flight 217 at 40%". Useless as a plan, priceless as a bound: no integer plan can ever beat it.

2 · Branch & bound

Don't round the fraction — split on it: one subproblem where flight 217 exists, one where it doesn't. Recurse into a tree. The LP bound kills entire subtrees: if a node's optimistic bound is already worse than the best plan found, nothing below it can win.

3 · …and price

Our LP has trillions of columns — too many to write, never mind solve. So start with a handful and ask an oracle ("pricing problem") for the single best missing column, repeatedly, until it certifies none is left. You solve the full LP while only ever touching a sliver of it. (§8–9 show how.)

4 · …and cut

Sometimes the LP exploits a loophole no integer plan could ("book cargo on 40% of a flight"). A cut is an extra inequality — true for every integer plan, false for the cheat — that slices the loophole off and tightens the bound. (§10 has a live one.)

Put together: branch-and-price-and-cut is a branch-and-bound tree (§11) where every node's LP is solved by column generation (§8) and tightened by cuts (§10). That's the machine of Act III — first the canvas it runs on, then each part in the order the solver uses them.

Act III · The machine, piece by piece

5One canvas for everything: the time-space network

To decide everything at once, everything must live in one mathematical place. That place is the time-space network: airports are horizontal lanes, time flows to the right across a periodic week (10,080 minutes that wrap Sunday→Monday), flights are diagonal arcs, and waiting on the ground is a horizontal arc. Both aircraft and cargo are walks on this canvas — that's what lets one model own all four decisions.

6The key idea: variables are stories, not switches

A naive model would use tiny variables — "does flight 217 exist?", "does shipment 4,182 use leg 88?" — and then need thousands of constraints just to keep the tiny decisions coherent (connectivity, timing, transfers, maintenance). ACSP-T does the opposite. Its variables are whole feasible stories, called columns:

Cargo path COLUMN

A complete, feasible itinerary for one O&D demand: legs + transfer stops, respecting availability time, delivery deadline, transfer minima, handling times, curfews. Variable xp = tonnes flowing on path p.

Flight string COLUMN

A complete, feasible work-week for one aircraft of one fleet: a chain of flights with turn times, returning to base, hitting an A-check within its interval. Variable ys = how many aircraft fly string s (integer).

Feasibility is baked into each column — an infeasible itinerary simply never becomes a variable. The model's constraints only need to arbitrate between stories: don't oversell capacity, don't use more aircraft than you own, cover every mandatory flight.

The price of this elegance: there are astronomically many feasible paths and strings — far too many to enumerate. The entire algorithm is a machine for working with that universe of columns without ever writing it down.

7The master problem: an LP that arbitrates

The Restricted Master Problem (RMP, in Rmp.cs) is a linear program over the columns generated so far ("restricted" = it only knows a tiny, growing subset of the universe). It maximizes profit — cargo revenue minus flying costs — subject to the paper's rows (14)–(22). Click each constraint family to see what it does:

Solving this LP yields two things: a plan (optimal values for the known columns) and — the real treasure — dual prices π on every row: what one extra unit of each shared resource is worth. A dual on a leg-capacity row says "a marginal tonne of lift on this leg is worth $310/t right now." Those prices are the language the next section speaks.

8Column generation: a conversation between an LP and two oracles

The RMP only knows a few columns — its optimum is a local truth. Are there better stories out there in the un-enumerated universe? To find out, we compute each hypothetical column's reduced cost: what it would add to profit at the current prices.

rc(path p) = revenue/t − Σ π_cap(legs of p) − π_dem(its O&D) − Σ π_cut rc(string s) = − operating cost(s) + Σ π over the flights s covers − π_fleet(its fleet) if rc > 0 for some column not yet in the RMP → adding it improves the LP if no such column exists anywhere → the LP optimum is PROVEN over all columns

Finding the best hypothetical column is the pricing problem — and here's the magic: it's a shortest-path problem on the time-space network, solvable without enumerating anything (§9). The loop below is the beating heart of the whole system. Step through it:

9Pricing: shortest paths through a priced world

PRICE-P — cargo paths A* + resources

For one O&D, imagine walking the time-space network from origin to destination. Each leg now has a toll: its capacity dual π_cap. The best new itinerary is the walk that maximizes revenue − tolls while respecting resources: the delivery deadline, transfer count, handling times. That's a Resource-Constrained Shortest Path Problem, solved with A* — an admissible estimate of the best possible remaining toll (h(v)) prunes hopeless prefixes, and dominance tests discard walks that are worse in both cost and remaining slack.

PRICE-S — flight strings labels over a multi-week DAG

Pricing an aircraft's work-week is harder: the walk must chain flights with turn times, wrap the periodic week, and fit an A-check within the maintenance interval. The pricer unrolls the periodic week into a multi-week DAG and runs a labeling algorithm: each label is a partial work-week carrying resources (elapsed time since maintenance, accumulated reduced cost). At each node, only the best σ = 20 labels survive (bucket ordering keeps them diverse rather than 20 near-clones).

This label limit is the algorithm's one honest approximation: without maintenance the string pricer is exact and so is the whole method; with maintenance, a provably-best string can occasionally be pruned — the same trade the paper makes (§9 of the paper).

10Cuts: closing the fractional loophole

LPs love loopholes. The selection variable of an optional flight can sit at x = 0.4 — flying "40% of a flight" — and cargo happily books 40% of its capacity, revenue with almost no fixed cost. Real airlines can't fly fractional airplanes, so the LP bound is too optimistic. Implied-bound cuts (paper §8) add the missing logic: flow on a flight's legs can't exceed capacity times its selection. Toggle the cut to watch the loophole close:

11Branching: forcing integer answers, without breaking pricing

Cuts tighten, but the LP can still land fractional. Then we split the world in two and solve both halves — recursively. The art is what to branch on. Branching on a single column ("forbid path #4,182") is a disaster: the pricer would just regenerate its near-twin. Instead we branch on decisions the pricers can respect, implemented as visibility flags on the pricing graphs (PricingRestrictions):

Flight in / out

Left child: the optional flight exists (x=1). Right child: it never happened (x=0) — its legs vanish from both pricing graphs.

Fleet assignment

This flight is flown by fleet k — or forbidden to fleet k. The string pricer for other fleets simply loses the flight from its DAG.

Follow-on

The finest scalpel: after flight i, an aircraft must (or must never) continue directly to flight j. Prunes fractional rotations pair by pair.

Act IV · What this repository adds

12Beyond the paper: our contributions

Everything in §§5–11 is the published science, implemented faithfully. But the paper's algorithm answers a narrower question than the business needs: it optimizes over a candidate list a human planner wrote, and at industry scale it can run for its whole time budget without producing a single feasible schedule. The layers below are this repository's own contributions — they are what turns the exact engine into the network-design results of §1.

The autonomous design loop OURS

The paper assumes a planner supplies the optional flights. We automated the planner: FlightProposer reads each round's solution — which demand had no feasible route at all, which had a route but was crowded out by capacity — ranks the gaps by revenue at risk, and generates candidate flights aimed at them (~80% hub round trips, 15% directs, 5% external charters, all priced from the airline's own cost structure). NetworkDesigner then runs the propose → solve → score → evict cycle until profit flattens. Step through a design run:

step 0 / 6
Round 0 — solve what you already fly. The base schedule (mandatory flights only) is solved to a baseline: $44.8M/week, and — just as important — a list of every O&D the solution left on the ground. That list is the fuel for round 1. (Trajectory illustrative of the integrator-scale GI-I run; fictional data.)

The cross-round machinery is where the engineering lives, and each piece exists because a measured failure demanded it: column-pool warm starts (round r re-injects round r−1's columns, so pricing only works on the new batch — root convergence in ~15–40 iterations instead of ~200), seeded incumbents (last round's schedule is feasible as-is in the new model, so a round can never lose money), eviction with amnesty (unused candidates leave the model but may be re-proposed later, when the grown network changes their economics), and a final rescue solve (every candidate that ever flew gets one joint chance with a longer clock, catching synergies split across batches).

Partitioning: how the engine reaches planet scale OURS

At 29,819 O&Ds (the fictional RLA instance) the exact engine drowns — not in theory, in wall-clock. Two partitioning techniques keep it honest while making the problem digestible:

Measured on RLA (29,819 O&Ds, full demand): the regional cycle gains +4–5M in 3–30 minutes of block time where the whole-network solver, seeded identically, gained +0 in 90 minutes — four consecutive runs. A 200–350-flight block adopts improvements in 4–50 seconds; the same heuristic stalls completely on the full master. Every merge is accepted only when the independently verified global profit improves, so the cycle is monotone by construction. An optional relay (pair passes) re-opens cross-region demand by solving region unions: a cross-border O&D enters the pair block whole, with exact windows, so feeder + trunk + distribution are decided in one model. On RLA the pairs ran clean but adopted nothing at the 3× contracting tariff — the third independent probe agreeing that the scattered cross-region tail is genuinely cheaper to contract — so pairs default off. In the web UI both live on one switch: regional split (and +pairs) next to the scenario picker.

The rest of our layer, at a glance

Restricted-master MIP heuristic

The paper only finds incumbents when an LP lands integral. At 1,250 flights that can mean 15 minutes and no schedule. We periodically re-solve all generated columns as a MIP: first feasible schedule in ~2–4 min instead of never.

OURS · price-and-branch, wired into the node loop

Local-branching adoption

On 29k-O&D models even the restricted MIP chokes. Searching within ≤ k selection flips of the incumbent (Fischetti–Lodi) made the first heuristic improvement this scale ever produced: +$3.8M in one round where the unrestricted heuristic moved $0.

OURS · tractable "best ≤ k changes" moves

Deliver-all mode

Integrators promise to deliver everything. Demand rows become equalities and a contracted-delivery column (3× own economics) guarantees completability — the model then minimizes the outsourcing bill. Every intermediate solution is feasible by construction.

OURS · service commitments, honestly priced

Farley bound, corrected

Hard per-round deadlines need a valid dual bound mid-convergence. We hit (and fixed) a subtle trap: columns already in the master at their upper bound legitimately price positive. Gaps went from a useless 2,016% to an honest 8.8% on the same run.

OURS · honest math under a clock

Independent feasibility audit

FeasibilityChecker lives outside the solver and re-validates every constraint on every incumbent. The solver throws rather than emit an infeasible schedule — trust is a feature.

OURS · plus 118 tests: pricers vs brute force, colgen vs full LP

Operational realism & engineering

Payload-range curves entering the LP exactly and linearly, night curfews, cargo handling times, editable airline-profile JSONs, an Excel round-trip for planners, dual LP backends (HiGHS / CPLEX, 1.5–3.4× faster), and a live web dashboard.

OURS · the difference between a solver and a tool

One more thing we're proud of that didn't work: a "hub waves" candidate class that proposed coordinated feeder→trunk→distribution bundles. Measured on the largest instance it changed nothing — the long tail of 2 t parcels genuinely doesn't beat contracted delivery once rotation fixed costs are paid. It stays in the codebase, tested and off by default, with the negative result documented. Honest measurement cuts both ways.

Why this architecture wins

Feasibility by construction

Deadlines, curfews, handling, maintenance live inside the pricers — the master never sees an infeasible story, and an independent checker audits every incumbent anyway.

Honest bounds, always

Column generation proves LP bounds over the full column universe; the Farley bound keeps them valid under hard time budgets. Gaps you can put in front of a CFO.

Scale by locality

Only promising columns are ever materialized; eviction bounds the design-round model; consolidation and regional solves stretch the same engine to planet-scale instances.
